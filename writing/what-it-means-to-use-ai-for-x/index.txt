6:T4756,<p>I've been thinking about AI, how we use it, and how well it could be used. Given all the recent progress, one has to wonder if and how&nbsp;AI could truly help people in a deep sense. Beyond convenience and settlement, towards flourishing.&nbsp;</p><p>I began trying to get to the nature of AI use and how it relates to what the user really wants, and I realized that this dynamic is a question about <em>technology</em> more generally.</p><p>Sometimes technologies are created to meet a need people have (refrigerators, medicines, transport, etc.). Other times, however, they are advertised as doing so, but the effect they have on people is actually very different (instagram for connecting, youtube for learning, etc). How can we make sense of this?</p><h2>The Gap Between 𝑥 and 𝑥̂</h2>
<p>A way of thinking about this that I like is that there is the need or goal that the person has, 𝑥; and then, there is what the tech actually addresses, 𝑥̂ (the effect it has on us).&nbsp;</p><p>Let's think of <em>youtube</em> in the context of <em>learning</em> as an example. Youtube will advertise their app as being good for a variety of things, like a great way to learn. Now a person can have the goal 𝑥=<span><em>"I want to be more educated"</em></span><span>, and use youtube for that purpose. But instead of learning, they spend hours scrolling shorts. Clearly, youtube is not serving them well. So, what is the relationship between 𝑥̂ and 𝑥?&nbsp;</span></p><p>𝑥̂ can be seen as a proxy of 𝑥&nbsp;<span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 1">[1*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">Of course, in some cases the approximation will be better than others</span></span>. Since it's an approximation, t<span>he gap between 𝑥 and 𝑥̂ is key. A larger gap indicates that the implementation of the tech is not fulfilling its purpose; there may be things left out that are very important to the need actually being addressed; sometimes the technology might go directly against other implicit requirements we had.&nbsp;</span></p><p><span>We can also think of an example where |𝑥 − 𝑥̂| is small. Imagine a digital version of a newspaper, and assume it is a newspaper you actually like reading. This is a perfectly fine use of technology because you are getting exactly what you want out of it (</span><span>knowing what is going on in the world)</span><span>. You are getting the news, and your goal is being fulfilled. 𝑥̂ ≈ 𝑥.</span></p>
<p>Now let's switch to social media, being sold as a way of connecting everyone. There is a big difference between the real, healthy connection that people want and the kind of connection they get from the technology (<span>|𝑥 − 𝑥̂|</span><span>&nbsp;is big). Here we tend to become more isolated, and end up getting precisely the kind of unhealthy behavior that we did not want from the tech.</span></p><p>So is all social media bad? Is youtube useless? Clearly not. There are situations and ways of using these products that can be largely beneficial (using instagram for your business account, or watching university lectures for instance). In the next section we will dive deeper into the dynamic between 𝑥 and 𝑥̂.</p>
<h2>5 key pieces of the puzzle</h2>
<p><span>Reflecting on these examples, we realize that the picture is not all black or white. To be concrete, I see five properties of the use of technology that seem to determine the gap between 𝑥 and 𝑥̂:</span></p>
<ul>
  <li>The <strong>need</strong> or goal the person actually has, and the <strong>proxy</strong> that the technology addresses (𝑥 and 𝑥̂).</li>
  <li>The <strong>design</strong> and appearance of the technology.</li><li><span>How </span><strong>flexible</strong><span> the technology is (what it's able to do).</span></li><li><span>How much </span><strong>agency</strong><span> we have to mold the use we make of it.</span></li>
  <li>The <strong>relationship</strong> we have with the technology (in an abstract sense).</li></ul><figure class="article-inline-figure article-inline-figure-compact"><img src="/images/technology-gap-dependency-diagram-final-v21.png" alt="Dependency diagram showing how a person’s underlying need or goal, the effect a technology has on its user, the relationship, the technology’s behavior, its design, flexibility, and user agency determine the gap between 𝑥 and 𝑥̂" width="2400" height="2580"><figcaption class="article-figure-caption">Figure 1. This diagram illustrates how the variables that affect 𝑥̂ influence each other. Crucially, agency is the main lever we have to make |𝑥 − 𝑥̂| small.&nbsp;</figcaption></figure><p><span>The effect that the tech has on us is determined by what the tech does, our relationship with it, and its design.&nbsp;</span>In some ways what the tech does is the most important aspect. If it makes us very effective at pursuing our goal, the design and relationship won't matter that much (how is your relationship with your fridge?).&nbsp;</p><p><span>The design and behavior determine whether we use the tech in a healthy or unhealthy way. Famous examples on the nefarious side include infinite scrolling, buttons that are pleasant to look at and press, and notifications that grab our attention. In those cases, it's very hard for us to resist overusing the app, and therefore our underlying goals are not realized. Instead of nurturing our curiosity and connecting with people in a deep way, we shorten our attention spans, become more isolated, and ultimately trade short-term gratification for long-term contentment.</span></p><p><span>But things don't have to stay this way. This is where agency comes in. Agency is (almost by definition) the lever we have access to for changing how technology affects us. We can primarily act to change both the functionality and the design of technology (which will in turn change our relationship with it). In software, examples include installing extensions to block certain aspects of an app, removing ads, or disabling notifications.</span></p><p><img src="/images/youtube-two-websites-v2.png" alt="Two side-by-side video platform designs: an attention-maximizing recommendation feed and a calmer interface designed for intentional learning" width="1672" height="941"></p><figure class="article-inline-figure"><figcaption class="article-figure-caption">Figure 2. Two versions of YouTube: one designed around engagement and one designed around intentional learning. I created this image with AI in 10 seconds. This goes to show how easy it is today to code up app extensions that make technology more aligned with our flourishing. Perhaps paradoxically, AI is a great tool to do this!</figcaption></figure><p>I depicted an example of what this could look like in practice in Figure 2. On the left we see what could be anyone's youtube page. Recommended videos, shorts, and a lot of ways for us to consume more content. On the right is a version where&nbsp;<span>the entire app is designed around learning, with&nbsp;</span><span>educational playlists converted into courses.&nbsp;</span><span>This example is&nbsp;</span><span>particularly relevant today</span><span>. We now live in a world where everyone has a lot more agency to tailor software products in whichever way they want, without needing to be expert software developers.&nbsp;</span></p><p><span>To me, this all means that technology cannot be judged only by what it does</span><span><span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 2">[2*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">On a moral note, one can absolutely be critical of how unhealthy an app's design and behavior are for the user by default. I believe it's more appropriate to direct the "good" or "bad" judgements to the people in charge of deciding such aspects of the implementation, but not to the technology itself</span></span>.&nbsp;</span>Its design, what it is optimized for, how powerful it is, and how much control we have over it all come as part of the package of what a piece of technology <em>is</em><span>.&nbsp;</span><span>Needless to say, all these aspects should be taken into account when developing new technology if we want it to be aligned with users on a deep, beneficial level.</span></p>
<h2>Implications for AI</h2>
<p>Now we can switch to AI and analyze it through the same lens. Interestingly, AI has two roles to play in this: one as a tool we can leverage to mold other technologies (like in the previous example), and another as a general assistant.</p>

<p>I previously explained how agency is the key lever we have, and how AI is what empowers us to have such control over software. I believe a vital factor f<span>or this potential to be distributed widely is <strong>openness</strong> <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-6">6</a>]</span>. Open-weight models <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-7">7</a>, <a href="#reference-8">8</a>, <a href="#reference-9">9</a>, <a href="#reference-10">10</a>]</span>, open harnesses&nbsp;</span><span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-3">3</a>, <a href="#reference-4">4</a>, <a href="#reference-5">5</a>]</span><span>&nbsp;and cheap access&nbsp;</span><span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 3">[3*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">There is obviously much more to say about what it would actually mean to democratize AI (risks, etc). This could be a post of its own</span></span><span>.</span></p>
<p>As an assistant, AI is an unusually flexible and powerful technology. Through prompting, tools, interfaces, and the harness around a model, it can do many different things very well and we can control a lot of how the system as a whole behaves (openness<span>&nbsp;is also very important here</span><span> </span><span class="hover-note"><span class="hover-note-marker">[4*]</span><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">Open knowledge means we can all learn from the best ways to use AI for our particular use case, how to make the most of it, and help us flourish</span></span><span>). In that sense, we have a considerable amount of agency to bend the initial offering towards what we actually want from it.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p>For example, if we look at AI companions, such as <a href="https://character.ai/">Character.AI</a>, what the AI is intended to be used for is to provide companionship. But it may be the case that, right now at least, AIs are very limited in their ability to provide real companionship on a deep level, and using them essentially results in unhealthy behaviors instead&nbsp;<span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-1">1</a>, <a href="#reference-2">2</a>]</span>.&nbsp;If what the people using these AIs truly want is to feel connected to other humans, the AI should not try to replace that. It should help them achieve that goal<span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 5">[5*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">For example, it could recommend how to connect with people. Or, if the person does not feel very confident in their social skills, they could do some practicing</span></span>.</p>
<p>Another example is writing. One could initially think that for anything that involves writing, one should just use AI because "it can do it so well". Indeed, an LLM can easily present an essay that is well-written, has a balanced set of points, is well-documented, etc. If, for whatever reason, the person only cares about the output, then this is a fine use of the technology, just like the digital newspaper was a fine use in the previous example.</p>
<p>What if instead, what the person actually wants is not so much the writing output, but they long for this deep state of flow where the act of writing itself is a very deep and fulfilling experience? What if what the person seeks is the friction, dealing with hard thoughts, investigating, that come with figuring it out on your own? That is a very different goal to achieve. In that case, the initial use of the technology is obviously the wrong one.&nbsp;<span>Instead, an LLM should help the person achieve those experiences specifically. As a matter of fact, it may be that the best way an LLM can be used to this end is to not be used at all<span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 6">[6*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">One can also get creative: the LLM can design a script that blocks all apps and distractions from the person's computer whenever they want to start writing</span></span>.</span></p>
<p><span>So before asking how AI can perform an activity, I think we should first ask what the person actually wants from that activity. What is the underlying need or goal? What proxy is the technology acting on instead? What does its design encourage? What kind of relationship will this create? And how can we leverage the agency we have to mold the entire system around the thing the person actually cares about?</span></p><p>As AIs are able to do more and become increasingly integrated into our lives, I think we should be especially careful not to over-rely on them. They should be studied not on their own, but as part of an entire system (Figure 1). Through deep, honest thinking, we should consider whether the best use of AI for us is “less AI,” “different AI,” or “no AI.”</p>
<h2>Parting Reflections</h2>
<p>One of the main realizations I came away with from writing this is how important agency is. I wonder<span>&nbsp;whether agency is temperamental, or if it's something that can be taught and learned (probably both). How does one become good at exercising agency? Can we help people develop it? Even though I have come across this term on social media, I now realize how important this question is (it&nbsp;</span><span>could easily be a post of its own,&nbsp;</span><span>maybe for the future!).</span></p>
<p>For my research, I'm interested in understanding the behavior and tendencies of AIs, and how they change depending on the harness and the HCI around them. What happens when we change the prompt, tools, memory, interface, or the amount of agency the person has? How should we evaluate the complete system and the effect it has on the person? And importantly, how do these systems compare with alternatives that use less AI or no AI at all <span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 7">[7*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">I keep reiterating this point of "no-AI" because a lot of research that I've read centers around 'how can AI be used to help this', and whether AI would be a good tool in the first place doesn't get much consideration</span></span>? Given that the capabilities of "base models" are already there,&nbsp;<span>I think the question is therefore not so much how to make AIs more powerful or how to fine-tune an LLM for something specific. The question is first to think deeply and honestly about how a system could truly benefit the person, and secondly, how to design the entire system around that</span><span>.</span></p>
<p>In a way, I think we are already there. Current models are incredibly capable and flexible, and the field is advancing super fast. We just need to be ready to benefit from the opportunities they make possible. A lot of work to do, but I remain optimistic!</p>
