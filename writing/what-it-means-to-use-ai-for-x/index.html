What It Means to Use AI for 𝑥

July 2026

𝑥 = a goal a person has.

I've been thinking about AI really helping people to achieve what they want—or, better said, actually helping them in the way they want to be helped.

I came to the conclusion that what all these different situations have in common is that there seems to be a mismatch between what the AI is initially deployed for, or what the person initially uses it for, and what the person actually desires on a deeper level.

I think the nature of the issue I'm talking about is generally about technologies being used to replace the final product in a superficial way, but in the process missing out on how that product is achieved. And that may be a very critical part of what the person actually wants to get out of the experience, goal, or activity.

One example is using technology to know what is going on in the world. Imagine a digital version of a newspaper—with all the necessary caveats, and assuming it is a newspaper you actually like reading. This is a perfectly fine use of technology because you are getting exactly what you want out of it. You are getting the news, and your goal is being fulfilled.

Now imagine a platform where a lot of people post what they think about the news, respond to each other, and argue with each other. One could argue that the actual output being produced is better than the digital newspaper, because you are being exposed to more viewpoints, more news, and simply more information. And since you wanted more information, well, there you have it, right?

But what if another goal—or another thing implicit in the goal the person has—is to try to make sense of the world in a healthy way and become a more thoughtful and cultured person? We can clearly imagine how this platform could be a very bad thing for that. In that sense, the technology is not a good fit. It is not designed in the right way for this person in this situation.

For example, if we look at AI companions, such as Character.AI, what the AI is intended to be used for is to provide companionship. But it may be the case that, right now at least, AIs are very limited in their ability to provide real companionship on a deep level, and instead result in basically unhealthy behaviors by the people who use them.

If what the people using these AIs truly want is to feel connection with other humans, the AI should not try to replace that. It should help them achieve that goal. For example, it could recommend how to connect with people. Or, if the person does not feel very confident in their social skills, they could do some practicing for a bit. Or it could provide advice in some way.

Another example is writing. AIs are used to write a lot. One could think that for anything that involves a person writing, one should just use AI because, quote-unquote, they can do it so well. An LLM can easily present an essay that is well-written, has a balanced set of points, is well-documented, etc. If, for whatever reason, the person only cares about the output, then this is a fine use of the technology, just like the digital newspaper was a fine use in the previous example.

What if instead, what the person using AI actually wants is not so much the writing output, but what they long for is this deep state of flow where writing is very enjoyable and a very deep and fulfilling experience? What if what the person truly wants out of writing is the friction, dealing with hard thoughts, investigating, and doing that yourself? That is a much different goal and thing to achieve. In that case, this implementation of the technology is obviously the wrong one and is not one that should be used.

Instead, an LLM should help the person achieve that specifically. And the answer may be that the best way an LLM can be used for this is to not be used.

This is just an example, but it could be the case that the LLM designs a script that blocks all apps and distractions from the person's computer whenever they want to start writing. That could be a good example of an LLM helping with what the person actually wants.

These are just a few examples, but one can quickly realize that this applies to pretty much anything a person wants to do with any technology.

As AI advances more and more quickly and these technologies are able to do more, I think it is very important not to lose focus, and to really consider deeply and thoughtfully the effect that this particular implementation of the technology as a whole—as an entire system—is having on the person, and whether that is the correct one or not.

I think the question is therefore not so much how to design new AIs, make them more powerful, or fine-tune an LLM to give directions for something specific. It is first to think deeply and honestly about how a system could help, and in what way, with the true thing the person wants. And secondly, how do you design the entire system around that? This is more the human-computer interaction side of things. Current models are already very capable and flexible, and through prompting and the harness around them we can control a lot of how the system as a whole behaves.
