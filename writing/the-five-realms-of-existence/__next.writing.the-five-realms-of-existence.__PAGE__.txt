4:T4415,<p>I had a great time studying ML algorithms at <a href="https://www.ucm.es/mathematics/" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer">university</a>. These are models that aim to capture patterns in data, so one important question is how do you come up with a model that will fit the phenomenon you care about. As a researcher, an even more important point is to first ask yourself what problem you care about, what is it that you want to solve and advance our collective understanding.</p>

<p>While I was in Munich developing computer vision algorithms to make cars drive on their own, I did a lot of revisiting old courses and looked at what the latest ML research was about. After studying and doing some introspection, I realized that one of the most important and cool things I could see myself working on is helping people reach their potential and live a full life. This is the question I keep coming back to when I ask myself what I want to do as a researcher. I want to understand what human fulfillment is, how we can get more of it, and what role should AI play (or not).</p>

<p>By <em>fulfillment </em>I'm referring to an umbrella term that aggregates wellbeing, happiness, flourishing, meaning, and other words we generally would use to describe life going well<span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 1">[1*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">I'm aware that there is no consensus on exactly how these concepts differ from each other <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-1">1</a>, <a href="#reference-2">2</a>]</span></span></span>.&nbsp;<span>Of course, this will mean different things to different people.</span></p>

<p>Why aren't we flourishing? Why isn't one particular person flourishing? Can we build an ML algorithm that will model this? How come we have all these powerful technologies <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-10">10</a>, <a href="#reference-11">11</a>, <a href="#reference-12">12</a>, <a href="#reference-13">13</a>, <a href="#reference-14">14</a>, <a href="#reference-15">15</a>]</span>, so much research <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-16">16</a>]</span>, yet so many people still feel they are not&nbsp;living their lives to the fullest? These are very complex questions, obviously, to which we don't have all the answers. However, I have come up with a way of making sense of these questions that I like quite a lot.</p>

<p>In this blog, I'll share this framework, which I'm calling the five realms of existence, showcase with a couple examples how it allows us to think about wellbeing situations in a much more holistic way, and share some thoughts on how AI could help. I don't intend this blog to be thorough, more an introduction to the nature of the questions I like to think about. I will likely write more about different research approaches or other thoughts in the future. Stay tuned!</p>





<h2>The Five Realms</h2>

<p>One way I have been thinking about wellbeing questions is through what I am calling different <em>realms of existence</em><span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 2">[2*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">I'm not completely sure about the name yet. The definitions are a bit fuzzy, and the realms clearly overlap. But for now, I find them useful to think about.</span></span>.

</p><ul><li>The&nbsp;<strong>cognitive</strong>: our inner voice and how we interpret and make sense of what happens to and around us.</li><li><strong>Relationships</strong>: friends, family, communities, and so on.</li><li>The<strong>&nbsp;environment</strong>: our home, culture, laws, institutions, technology, and other systems around us.</li><li><strong>Activities</strong>: what we do and how we spend our day-to-day (habits, etc).</li><li>The <strong>biological</strong>: genetics, physiology, sleep, nutrition, etc.</li></ul>

<p>Note that these are not cleanly separated boxes. Religion, for example, could be part of someone’s cognition (sense of meaning), part of their relationships (community), and part of the wider culture and environment in which they live.</p>

<p>What I like about this perspective is that it allows us to think about a wide variety of situations a person might be in a more <strong>holistic</strong> way.</p>

<h2>Five Realms in Action</h2>

<p>Suppose, for example, that according to a theory of wellbeing such as PERMA <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-3">3</a>]</span>, Dave seems to be struggling with engagement. Turns out he spends several hours a day&nbsp;on his phone. At first glance, one might think that this is clearly a behavioral issue, and that the solution is to make Dave delete the habit of being on his phone so much, and replace it instead with something healthier, like reading. This is not the full story though:</p>

<p>There is the <strong>activity itself</strong>: the habit of being on the phone.</p>

<p>But there is also the <strong>environment</strong>:&nbsp;a culture shaped by consumerism and surrounded by technologies where billions have been invested to capture and retain our attention <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-4">4</a>, <a href="#reference-5">5</a>]</span><span>.&nbsp;</span></p>

<p>Then there is the <strong>cognitive side</strong>: the person’s relationship with the phone and what using it means to them. It may be fun, offer comfort, or connection, all mingled together.</p>

<p>Looking at these different aspects gives us a more holistic understanding of what may be happening, and allows us to make more informed decisions to ultimately improve that person's wellbeing<span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 3">[3*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">Perhaps changing the relationship the person has to their phone first would automatically change behavior; it could also be that on a more macro-scale, new policies could be implemented to restrict or nudge how people use screens (these are just examples).</span></span>.</p>

<p>Another telling example is exercise, one of the pillars of good health <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-6">6</a>]</span>.&nbsp;<span>Exercising is first an activity, b</span><span>ut the environment is also important. How does the person "understand" exercise? What does it mean for them? Is it seen as something enjoyable, necessary, embarrassing, or unpleasant? Does the person have a good gym nearby or would they have to exercise in a cramped apartment? How would it fit into their schedule?&nbsp;</span><span>And then the cognitive side: perhaps the person has never exercised and does not see themselves as someone who can do it, maybe they feel insecure or intimidated, or perhaps they are already very motivated.</span></p>

<p>In some situations, the person is on board and motivated and building the habit is the most important; in others, taking good care of the cognitive aspects concurrently would be most effective.</p><h2>Where Could AI Help?</h2>

<p>This five-realm approach also allows us to think more clearly about how AI<span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 4">[4*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">By "AI", I mean the systems we currently have (LLMs/chatbots/agents, image and video generation models, etc), but also possible future systems, like omnipresent AI devices and robots</span></span> could fit in this picture.</p>

<p>Let’s return to the screen time example.&nbsp;<span>An AI could simply tell the person: <em>"Hi Dave, I think you should spend less time on your phone".</em>&nbsp;</span><span>That's probably not going to help very much. In all seriousness, its effect would depend on how the advice is presented, how the person processes it, what they think of AI in general, and what kind of relationship they have with the system.</span></p>

<p>Alternatively, AI could analyze how the person spends time on their phone by looking at screen usage statistics and extracting patterns, etc. It could help them develop a reasonable plan (blocking particular apps from Monday to Friday or something) and then, after the person approves the plan, implement it (without the user even needing to know how to print 'hello world').&nbsp;<span>Now the AI is not offering advice, but enabling the person to have control over their environment.</span></p>

<p><span>For exercise, AI could design a routine, track someone’s lifts, how often they go to the gym, etc (tackle the activity realm). Or it could create motivational videos or guide the person through an introspective exercise around their motivation and identity (tackle the cognitive realm).</span></p>

<p><span>And these examples are still quite narrow.&nbsp;</span><span>Suppose we care about helping people feel less isolated and more connected using AI. One initial response is to imagine an AI companion ("let’s use it to solve the problem directly"). But perhaps an important part of the issue is environmental: our laws or public spaces may not be conducive to people spending time together and forming relationships <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-7">7</a>, <a href="#reference-8">8</a>]</span></span><span>. </span><span>AI could help us study whether those systems are working against us, then analyze policies, model alternatives, and work with policymakers to design environments that are more conducive to human-to-human connection.</span></p>

<p>Thinking beyond AI, computational models in general can also help us improve our understanding of the phenomena where&nbsp;<span>our current knowledge may not yet tell us which interventions work best, for whom, or how reliably they generalize <span class="inline-citation">[<a href="#reference-9">9</a>]</span>. Here, computational models can eventually help us understand these processes more deeply<span class="hover-note"><sup class="hover-note-marker" tabindex="0" aria-label="Show note 5">[5*]</sup><span class="hover-note-card" role="note">That new understanding might later be integrated into an activity, used by a human practitioner, inform public policy, or guide an AI system that works directly with a person</span></span>.&nbsp;</span><span>This connects to a broader question I have been thinking about: what does it really mean to use AI for something that involves a goal a person has? How should an AI help in writing? You should ask, what are you truly after? The AI should help with </span><em>that</em><span>.&nbsp;I explore this more in depth in </span><a href="/writing/what-it-means-to-use-ai-for-x"><em>What It Means to Use AI for 𝑥</em></a><span>.</span></p>

<h2>How I Want to Approach This</h2>

<p>These are the kinds of questions I like spending time thinking about and what I want to dedicate my research to. This is also, at least for now, how I make sense of the complex field of wellbeing and the possibilities in front of us.</p><p>As I mentioned before, although I intentionally made this blog introductory, I will likely write more about research approaches involving AI and wellbeing. I have mentioned one earlier, two more I am thinking about are about "precision wellbeing" and "harness exploration" (in the context of LLMs). I'd also like to do a post on a high-level overview of the wellbeing research, and how it connects to the field of AI broadly. As far as my research goes, I'm excited to explore how AI agents can promote wellbeing, how far can prompting go, and how to make models good at unverifiable tasks.</p>

<p>I'll conclude with a few principles on how I want to approach the research I do:</p>

<p>First, I don't want to tie myself to one computational method, model, or data modality. The technology is advancing so fast that the best thing to do is to be open, adapt quickly, and use whatever tools are best for the problem at hand.</p>

<p>Second, I want the research to remain closely linked to real problems whose solution would benefit people.&nbsp;</p>

<p>Third, I want to study not only illness, crisis, etc. Those problems matter enormously, but there is also room to study positive psychology, growth, fulfillment, and how an ordinary life can become a fuller one.</p>
